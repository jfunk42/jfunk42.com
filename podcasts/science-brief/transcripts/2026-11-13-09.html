Space Assembly vs. Lunar Moratorium: The Governance of Off-World Infrastructure

HOST: Welcome back to jFunk42 Science Brief, where we bring you the latest breakthroughs and crucial debates shaping the scientific world. I am your host, and today we are looking out into space—specifically at how humanity plans to build large-scale infrastructure beyond Earth, and whether our legal and policy frameworks are anywhere near ready for what is coming. For decades, space exploration was defined by small probes, tightly packed satellites, and lightweight capsules designed to fit inside the narrow nose cones of Earth-launched rockets. But as our ambitions grow, we are hitting severe physical limits. If we want to construct massive space stations, solar power stations, or deep-space habitats, we cannot simply pack them onto a standard rocket and launch them fully formed. The physics of spaceflight will not allow it. That is why automated assembly directly in space, through pioneering initiatives like NOM4D, is being developed as the vital pathway forward. At the same time, efforts to return humans to the lunar surface are accelerating under NASA's Artemis program, leaning heavily on commercial partnerships like Blue Origin's Blue Moon Mark 2 lander, led by lander integration experts like Katie Spicer at NASA. But as major global powers and private corporations prepare to construct habitats and harvest resources on the Moon, a growing chorus of international policy scholars is raising a loud red flag. They argue that the Moon is being treated like an unregulated frontier, and they are calling for an immediate moratorium on lunar industrialization until global governance can catch up. COUNTERPOINT: It is a fascinating juxtaposition. On one hand, you have engineering teams pushing hard against the stubborn laws of physics to enable large-scale structures and routine lunar landings. On the other hand, you have political scientists asking whether we ought to slow down before we create an irreversible wild west in orbit and on the lunar surface. The governance perspective is not just about slowing down technology for the sake of bureaucracy; it is about addressing deep structural gaps. In a recent research article titled "Don't Fly Me to the Moon: A Moratorium on Lunar Industrialization," published in the journal Global Environmental Politics, researchers Florian Rabitz from the Vilnius University Institute of International Relations and Political Science and Xiao-Shan Yap from Utrecht University lay out a detailed critique of our current trajectory. They warn that as major powers and private enterprises race to build lunar infrastructure and exploit off-world resources, we are plunging ahead without clear frameworks for environmental protection, international oversight, or fair distribution of benefits. So while the engineering breakthroughs in automated manufacturing and commercial lander integration are undeniable, we have to look closely at the framework guiding those capabilities before we deploy them at scale. HOST: That balance between engineering capability and policy oversight is exactly what makes this moment in space exploration so critical. To understand why automated assembly in space is becoming necessary in the first place, we have to talk about the physical constraints of launching objects from Earth. Every spacecraft launched from Earth faces two overwhelming engineering bottlenecks: the rocket fairing and the rocket equation. The rocket fairing is the protective shroud at the top of a launch vehicle that shields the payload from atmospheric pressure and heating during ascent. No matter how large your rocket is, the interior dimensions of that fairing impose a hard physical ceiling on the diameter and volume of anything you want to carry into space. If your structural component or antenna dish is wider than the fairing, you simply cannot launch it as a single solid piece. COUNTERPOINT: And folding those structures up inside a fairing introduces massive engineering risk. Engineers spend years designing intricate origami-like deployment mechanisms—hinges, motors, joints, and latching systems—just to allow a large structure to unfurl once it reaches orbit. Every single hinge or moving part becomes a potential point of failure. If one joint jams in the vacuum of space, an entire multi-million or multi-billion dollar mission can be compromised. HOST: Exactly. And that brings us to the second, even more unforgiving constraint: the rocket equation. The rocket equation dictates that to accelerate a given mass of payload into orbit, you must carry a exponentially larger mass of fuel. But that fuel itself has mass, which requires even more fuel to lift, creating a harsh law of diminishing returns. You cannot simply build a bigger rocket to lift an arbitrarily massive, fully constructed steel or composite station from the ground. The energy requirements quickly become prohibitive. This is where automated in-space assembly, as demonstrated by the NOM4D initiative, fundamentally changes the calculus. Instead of launching giant, completed, fragile structures, NOM4D focuses on sending raw, compact materials or modular sub-components into orbit, where specialized automated systems assemble them directly in the environment where they will operate. By constructing hardware in space, you completely bypass atmospheric drag and fairing size restrictions. You can build structures that are hundreds of meters wide—far larger than anything that could ever fit inside a launch vehicle shroud. COUNTERPOINT: That technical logic is sound from a pure physics standpoint, but let us look at the structural consequences of that shift. When initiatives like NOM4D demonstrate that automated systems can manufacture and assemble massive structures directly in orbit or on the lunar surface, they remove the primary physical barrier that previously constrained space industrialization. Historically, high launch costs and small payload volumes served as a natural speed bump, limiting how much material humans could place into orbit or on the Moon. If automated assembly removes those physical limits, the pace of industrial activity can scale up exponentially. And that brings us right back to the core warning raised by Florian Rabitz and Xiao-Shan Yap. Their research highlights that international policy and space law are completely unprepared for a world where large-scale, automated manufacturing occurs outside Earth's atmosphere. Without enforceable international agreements on resource rights and structural standards, automated assembly tools like NOM4D could end up being used by whichever nation or corporation gets there first to stake out dominance over prime orbital slots or lunar surface territory. HOST: That is a fair point regarding the speed of deployment, but we are also seeing this push for sustainable off-world infrastructure play out right now in concrete, multi-partner programs like NASA's Artemis program. Artemis is designed to establish a long-term, sustainable human presence on and around the Moon, and it relies heavily on integrating advanced commercial hardware into its core mission architecture. A primary example of this is the development of commercially provided Human Landing Systems. NASA is working directly with commercial partners, including Blue Origin, which is developing the Blue Moon Mark 2 lander. The operational integration of these complex systems requires dedicated leadership. At NASA, Katie Spicer serves as the NASA lander integration lead for Blue Origin's Blue Moon Mark 2 lander. Her work involves ensuring that private sector engineering seamlessly interfaces with NASA's broader Artemis architecture, safety standards, and operational goals. This public-private integration model proves that off-world development is not just an abstract theoretical exercise; it is an active, highly structured engineering effort moving toward routine lunar landings. COUNTERPOINT: The role of integration leads like Katie Spicer highlights how deeply entwined public space agencies have become with private aerospace corporations. But while the engineering integration between NASA and private contractors like Blue Origin is proceeding with great technical discipline, the overarching legal governance framing those operations remains deeply disputed on the international stage. In their paper published in Global Environmental Politics, Rabitz and Yap argue that commercial landing systems like the Blue Moon Mark 2 are part of a broader trend where major spacefaring powers and private entities are going "full tilt" to establish physical infrastructure and prepare for resource extraction on the lunar surface. Their research argues that this rapid expansion is happening in a legal vacuum. While individual national programs create technical safety standards for their own landers, there is no universally accepted international framework regulating who has the right to extract lunar resources, how nearby operations must maintain environmental protection, or how the economic benefits of those resources should be shared globally. HOST: Let us break down those specific concerns raised by Rabitz and Yap, because their call for a moratorium rests on three main pillars: environmental protection, international regulation, and benefit-sharing. From an operational perspective, why do they argue that a moratorium is necessary right now, rather than allowing commercial landers and automated assembly to develop under existing treaty structures? COUNTERPOINT: The core argument from Rabitz and Yap is that existing international space law—primarily built upon foundational treaties from the mid-twentieth century—was designed for an era when space exploration was conducted exclusively by a small handful of nation-states performing scientific exploration, not private companies and state alliances planning commercial scale industrialization. In "Don't Fly Me to the Moon: A Moratorium on Lunar Industrialization," the authors argue that the Moon should not be treated as a "Wild West" where the first entity to set up a commercial lander or an automated manufacturing module gets to claim exclusive operational zones or exploit valuable local resources like water ice. First, consider environmental protection. The lunar surface has a delicate, pristine scientific environment, including ancient regolith and volatile ice deposits in permanently shadowed craters that contain billions of years of solar system history. Heavy industrial activity, rocket exhaust contamination from landing systems, and mining operations could permanently disrupt or erase those pristine scientific records before researchers even have the chance to study them. HOST: But advocates for lunar development would counter that extracting resources in-situ—such as mining water ice from the lunar surface to produce hydrogen and oxygen fuel—is actually vital for environmental and operational sustainability in space. If you can harvest water on the Moon to make rocket propellant, you do not have to launch all that heavy fuel off Earth's surface, which drastically reduces the environmental cost and energy budget of Earth launches. Isn't in-situ resource utilization an essential stepping stone for any long-term presence in space? COUNTERPOINT: It may be a stepping stone for mission architecture, but Rabitz and Yap's point is about governance and equity. Who owns that water ice? Who gets to decide which craters are preserved for science and which are handed over to commercial firms for propellant production? Under current dynamics, the nations and corporations that possess advanced hardware—like commercial landing systems or automated manufacturing platforms—will naturally capture the most valuable sites on the Moon. This brings us to their second and third pillars: international regulation and benefit-sharing. The authors point out that if resource extraction proceeds under a first-come, first-served model, it exacerbates global inequalities. Non-spacefaring nations will be completely excluded from the economic and technological dividends of lunar resources. Rabitz and Yap argue that without a pause—a formal moratorium on lunar industrialization—the world will default to a regime of competitive resource grabbing, leading to geopolitical conflict and the degradation of the lunar environment. They contend that we must establish binding, multilateral regulations *before* industrial infrastructure becomes permanently entrenched. HOST: A moratorium sounds clean on paper, but how realistic is it to halt technological progress when hardware is already being built and tested? Engine integration, lander assembly, and automated manufacturing systems are already well underway. Katie Spicer and the teams working on the Blue Moon Mark 2 lander are actively solving complex technical interfaces to ensure human safety and mission success for Artemis. Programs like NOM4D are solving fundamental physics problems to ensure that future space structures can be built efficiently. If you impose a blanket moratorium on lunar industrialization, don't you risk freezing the very innovation required to make space operations safer, cleaner, and more efficient? COUNTERPOINT: That is the classic tension between rapid technological deployment and precautionary governance. But a moratorium does not necessarily mean stopping all scientific research or basic engineering development. What researchers like Rabitz and Yap are proposing is a pause on commercial scale resource extraction and permanent industrial claims until international consensus is reached. They argue that if you let commercial deployment run ahead of policy, the economic interests will become so powerful that effective regulation will become impossible after the fact. Look at history on Earth: once industrial infrastructure is established in a common pool resource environment, retrofitting strict environmental controls or benefit-sharing mandates is notoriously difficult. By raising these questions now, in peer-reviewed policy journals like Global Environmental Politics, researchers are trying to force international bodies to sit down and negotiate explicit rules for governance. They want to ensure that the Moon is treated as a shared global heritage rather than an unregulated economic free-for-all. HOST: That critique highlights why this debate is so multifaceted. On the technical side, the physical limits of getting into space demand radical innovation. We cannot move past our current limitations without technologies like automated in-space assembly. NOM4D shows us a path where we can build unprecedented structures directly in orbit, bypassing the severe constraints of rocket fairing volumes and the payload restrictions imposed by the rocket equation. Furthermore, the Artemis program demonstrates how public-private integration—led by dedicated professionals like Katie Spicer integrating Blue Origin's Blue Moon Mark 2 lander—can translate ambitious space exploration goals into functional operational hardware. The engineering drive to solve these hard physical problems is moving at an incredible pace. COUNTERPOINT: And on the policy side, the research by Florian Rabitz and Xiao-Shan Yap serves as a vital reality check. They remind us that solving the engineering challenges of launch fairings, fuel ratios, and lander integration is only half the battle. If we deploy those technical solutions into a policy vacuum, we risk turning the Moon into a arena of unregulated competition, environmental degradation, and geopolitical friction. Their proposed moratorium on lunar industrialization is a direct challenge to decision-makers to prioritize international regulation, environmental safeguards, and equitable benefit-sharing alongside hardware development. HOST: Ultimately, these two perspectives represent the dual engines of modern space exploration: the imperative to innovate and the responsibility to govern. As automated assembly technologies like NOM4D mature and commercial landers like the Blue Moon Mark 2 prepare to take humans back to the lunar surface, the dialogue between aerospace engineers and policy researchers will determine not just *how* we reach the stars, but what kind of society we build when we get there. COUNTERPOINT: Indeed. Balancing technical audacity with rigorous global governance is perhaps the most difficult engineering challenge of all. HOST: That is all for today's jFunk42 Science Brief.
