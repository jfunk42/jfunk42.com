For the first couple of billion years of Earth's existence, the planet was a relatively quiet place biologically, dominated entirely by simple, single-celled organisms. But then, a dramatic transformation occurred. Complex cells, known as eukaryotes, emerged. These are cells with a defined nucleus and specialized internal machinery, the evolutionary ancestors of all plants, animals, and fungi. For decades, the leading theory has been that this evolutionary leap was intimately tied to the rise of oxygen in Earth's atmosphere and oceans. One study, analyzing ancient rocks in Australia, provides massive, direct fossil evidence that early complex life was strictly tied to oxygenated environments. On the other hand, we have geochemical signatures—chemical records locked in minerals that tell us about the global environment, the atmosphere, and the cycles of carbon and oxygen. In these high-energy, chemically rich environments, specialized microorganisms known as methane-eating microbes can thrive. As these microbes consume the methane, they process the carbon, utilizing specific isotopes of carbon preferentially. This highly specific biological processing, occurring under the influence of magmatic heat and hydrocarbon fluids, leaves behind a distinct isotopic carbon signature in the newly formed minerals. Because this signature is so stark and unusual, when scientists first discovered it in rocks dating back two billion years, they assumed it must represent a planetary-scale event—a massive, global change in Earth's carbon cycle linked to a major atmospheric oxygenation event. But what this new research demonstrates is that you don't need a global atmospheric revolution to create this signature. You just need a local intrusion of magma, some hydrocarbons, and a community of methane-eating microbes working together in the deep subsurface.
