High-Energy Windows to the Violent Universe

Imagine stepping outside on a clear, moonless night far from the glow of city lights. As you look up at the dark canopy of the night sky, the universe appears peaceful, silent, and largely unchanging. The stars seem like permanent, quiet fixtures, shining with a steady and comforting light. But this serene view is a beautiful illusion. It is a limitation of our biology, because the human eye can only detect a tiny sliver of the electromagnetic spectrum. We see visible light, which is just a single, narrow octave in the vast symphony of cosmic radiation. If we could expand our vision to perceive the highest-energy wavelengths of the spectrum, the night sky would transform from a quiet sanctuary into a theater of extreme violence, dense gravitational traps, and explosive cosmic events. In this invisible, high-energy universe, dead stellar remnants spin hundreds of times per second, massive giant stars shed their outer layers in violent torrents, and colliding stellar corpses trigger flashes of light that can briefly outshine entire galaxies. This is the domain of X-rays and gamma-rays. Unlike visible light, which is produced by the gentle thermal glowing of stars like our Sun, X-rays and gamma-rays are born from the most extreme physical environments imaginable. They are generated by matter heated to tens of millions of degrees, by particles accelerated to nearly the speed of light, and by cosmic explosions of unfathomable proportions. Fortunately, we do not have to remain blind to this extreme universe. Over the last several decades, astrophysics has entered a golden age of space-based observation. Because Earth's atmosphere shieldingly blocks these high-energy rays—protecting life on the ground but blinding astronomers on Earth—we must send our instruments into space. Today, we are going to explore how three distinct, cutting-edge space missions are pulling back the veil on this invisible, high-energy cosmos. We will examine the Japan-led XRISM observatory and its direct observations of a stellar wind thief, look under the hood of the meticulously engineered COSI telescope as it comes together, and preview the upcoming StarBurst mission, which is set to hitch a ride into orbit to investigate the origins of heavy elements and some of the briefest, most powerful explosions in the cosmos. Our journey begins in the realm of X-rays with the Japan-led X-ray Imaging and Spectroscopy Mission, also known as XRISM. Developed as a collaborative effort involving international partners like NASA, XRISM is a state-of-the-art space observatory designed to study the hottest regions of the universe. It does this not just by taking pictures, but by performing incredibly precise spectroscopy. In astronomy, a spectrometer acts like a high-tech prism, splitting the incoming light into its individual energy levels or wavelengths. By studying these spectral lines, scientists can determine the temperature, velocity, and chemical composition of gas that is millions of light-years away. It is like taking the cosmic temperature of the most extreme environments in the universe. Recently, astronomers using data from XRISM made a direct, highly significant observation that solved a long-standing puzzle about how compact stellar objects feed on their neighbors. They pointed XRISM at a binary system where a massive, giant star is orbited by a compact companion. In this case, that compact companion is a pulsar. A pulsar is a neutron star—the collapsed, ultra-dense core left behind after a massive star runs out of fuel and dies in a supernova explosion. Pulsars are famous for having incredibly strong magnetic fields and for rotating rapidly, emitting beams of electromagnetic radiation from their magnetic poles like cosmic lighthouses. When a pulsar is locked in an orbital dance with a massive giant star, the relationship is far from peaceful. Massive giant stars are not stable, quiet objects. They are constantly losing mass, blowing their outer layers into space in a powerful, high-velocity stream called a stellar wind. This stellar wind is vastly more powerful than the solar wind emitted by our Sun. It carries a tremendous amount of gas and charged particles outward into the stellar system. As this stellar wind flows outward from the giant star, it encounters the intense gravitational field of the nearby pulsar. Because a pulsar compresses the mass of an entire star into a sphere roughly the size of a city, its surface gravity is unimaginably strong. XRISM was able to directly observe the pulsar gathering and capturing this outflowing stellar wind. Instead of the wind simply escaping into the deep vacuum of space, the pulsar acts as a massive gravitational vacuum cleaner, pulling the giant star’s gas into its own gravitational well. As the captured gas falls toward the pulsar, it accelerates to extreme speeds. When particles in the gas collide at these velocities, they create immense friction, heating the gas to millions of degrees. At these extreme temperatures, the gas begins to glow intensely in X-rays. XRISM’s observations directly demonstrated that this captured stellar wind acts as the primary power source for strong, unpredictable X-ray flares. These flares are massive bursts of high-energy light that occur when dense clumps of the captured wind crash down onto the pulsar, releasing sudden cascades of energy. This discovery is a major milestone for NASA’s exploration of the extreme universe. It gives scientists a front-row seat to the physics of accretion—the process by which compact objects grow by pulling in surrounding material. Understanding how a pulsar captures a stellar wind helps theorists refine their models of how matter behaves under the influence of extreme gravity and intense magnetic fields, environments that we could never hope to replicate in a laboratory on Earth. But X-rays represent only the lower-energy boundary of the violent universe. If we push even higher up the electromagnetic spectrum, we leave X-rays behind and enter the regime of gamma-rays. Gamma-rays are the most energetic form of light in existence, carrying thousands to millions of times more energy than X-rays. Because gamma-ray photons are so energetic, they do not behave like the light we are familiar with. They cannot be reflected or focused by traditional mirrors or lenses. If a gamma-ray photon hits a standard glass mirror, it will not bounce off; instead, it will pass straight through, or collide violently with the atoms in the glass, destroying the material. To detect and map these high-energy photons, scientists have to build entirely different kinds of detectors. One of the most highly anticipated new instruments designed for this task is the Compton Spectrometer and Imager, or COSI. COSI is a space telescope being developed to observe gamma-rays in the mega-electronvolt, or MeV, energy range. This specific energy range is incredibly important because it is where we can observe the signatures of radioactive decay from elements forged in supernova explosions, allowing us to map where the building blocks of planets and life are being created across the galaxy. Building an instrument capable of measuring these highly penetrating gamma-rays is a monumental engineering challenge. Because you cannot focus gamma-rays with a mirror, you must instead measure how they interact with the detector itself. This requires an incredibly precise and complex assembly of high-purity detector crystals and ultra-fast electronics. Recently, the COSI mission hit a major, tangible milestone in its physical construction. On July 8, 2026, engineers and managers at the University of California, Berkeley, gathered around an assembly table. They watched with immense focus as the COSI detector assembly was slowly and carefully raised from the table, a critical step in integrating the telescope’s core hardware. If you were to look at the top of the COSI detector box, you would see four distinct pieces of silver material. These silver covers are not just cosmetic; they serve a vital protective purpose. They cover and protect the delicate flex circuits of the instrument. Flex circuits are flexible printed electronic circuits that can bend and conform to tight, complex spaces, allowing engineers to route wiring through the highly compact interior of a modern spacecraft. In the COSI detector, these flex circuits play an absolutely critical role. When a gamma-ray photon enters the detector, it collides with electrons inside the detector crystals. This process is known as Compton scattering. During the collision, the photon transfers a portion of its energy to an electron and deflects at an angle, continuing on to collide with other electrons inside the detector. By measuring the exact positions and energy deposits of these successive collisions, the telescope can mathematically reconstruct the original path and energy of the incoming gamma-ray photon. This allows COSI to perform both spectroscopy and imaging of gamma-ray sources without needing traditional mirrors. However, the electrical signals generated by these microscopic collisions inside the detector are incredibly weak. If these signals are degraded, delayed, or subject to electromagnetic interference on their way to the computer, the telescope’s data becomes useless. The flex circuits are the physical pathways that carry these highly sensitive signals from the detectors directly to the readout electronics. The readout electronics then amplify and digitize the signals, converting the chaotic interactions of cosmic gamma-rays into clean, structured data that scientists can analyze. The silver covers protect these critical electronic pathways from physical damage, dust, and electrical interference during the assembly and testing process, ensuring that the instrument will perform flawlessly once it reaches the harsh environment of space. While COSI is being built to perform broad, detailed surveys of the gamma-ray sky, another upcoming space science mission is being designed with a much more targeted, rapid-response objective. This is the StarBurst mission. StarBurst is a small satellite designed to detect and study some of the most dramatic, explosive events in the history of the cosmos: neutron star mergers and the origins of short gamma-ray bursts. A neutron star merger is an event of cataclysmic proportions. It occurs when two neutron stars—each containing more mass than our Sun compressed into an area the size of a city—orbiting closely in a binary system eventually lose orbital energy and collide. The resulting impact is so violent that it literally deforms the fabric of space-time, sending out ripples known as gravitational waves. At the same time, the collision tears a portion of the neutron stars apart, spraying super-dense, neutron-rich material out into the surrounding space. In the fiery aftermath of this collision, the neutrons are rapidly captured by atomic nuclei, forging heavy elements like gold, platinum, and lead. In fact, astrophysical models suggest that the vast majority of the heavy elements in the universe, including the precious metals we find on Earth, were created in these massive stellar collisions. But the story doesn't end there. As the two neutron stars merge, they also trigger a tremendous explosion that launches a jet of plasma outward at nearly the speed of light. This jet produces an incredibly intense, highly concentrated beam of gamma-ray radiation known as a short gamma-ray burst. These bursts are called "short" because they typically last for less than two seconds. Despite their brief duration, a short gamma-ray burst releases more energy in those couple of seconds than our Sun will emit over its entire ten-billion-year lifespan. Because these bursts are so brief and occur in random, unpredictable locations in the sky, detecting them is a constant race against time. To catch them, you need a dedicated, highly sensitive satellite that can monitor a wide view of the cosmos and instantly alert other telescopes when a burst is detected. This is the precise mission of StarBurst. By detecting the high-energy gamma-rays from these short bursts, StarBurst will act as an early-warning system, allowing astronomers to coordinate observations with other space telescopes and ground-based observatories to study the fading glow of the merger across multiple wavelengths of light. To get this critical scientific instrument into space, NASA has officially selected SpaceX to provide launch services. StarBurst is scheduled to launch aboard a Falcon 9 rocket as part of a Bandwagon rideshare mission. The launch will take place from Space Launch Complex 40 at the Cape Canaveral Space Force Station in Florida. The choice of a Bandwagon rideshare mission represents a fascinating shift in how modern space science is conducted. Historically, sending a scientific satellite into space was an incredibly expensive endeavor that required purchasing a dedicated rocket. This meant that small, highly focused missions often struggled to find funding or had to wait years for a launch opportunity. SpaceX’s rideshare program, particularly the Bandwagon missions, acts like a high-tech carpool for space. A single, powerful Falcon 9 rocket carries a primary payload into orbit, but has leftover capacity to carry several smaller, independent satellites at the same time. The Bandwagon missions are specifically designed to place multiple small satellites into mid-inclination orbits. For a mission like StarBurst, a mid-inclination orbit is highly advantageous because it allows the satellite’s gamma-ray detectors to regularly scan a large fraction of the sky, maximizing the chances of catching a brief, random short gamma-ray burst. By using a commercial rideshare launch, NASA can deploy a cutting-edge, highly targeted science instrument like StarBurst at a fraction of the cost of a traditional dedicated launch, accelerating our ability to answer fundamental questions about the origin of elements in the universe. When we look at XRISM, COSI, and StarBurst together, we see a beautiful, interconnected picture of modern astrophysics. These are not isolated, redundant projects. They represent a highly coordinated, multi-faceted assault on the mysteries of the high-energy universe, with each mission bringing its own specialized tools to the table. XRISM, with its advanced X-ray spectrometer, allows us to study the relatively slow, continuous processes of the high-energy sky—such as how a dense pulsar pulls in and feeds upon the stellar wind of its giant companion star over long periods of time. This gives us a detailed, high-resolution look at the physics of gravity and accretion in binary systems. Meanwhile, COSI, which is coming together through the precise engineering of its detector assembly and flex circuits at UC Berkeley, is designed to observe medium-energy gamma-rays. It will map the radioactive ashes of dead stars across the Milky Way, helping us understand the long-term chemical evolution of our galaxy and how the elements that make up our world are distributed over millions of years. And finally, StarBurst, with its upcoming ride into orbit on a SpaceX Falcon 9 from Cape Canaveral, will focus on the most transient, sudden events in the cosmos. It will watch for the briefest flashes of high-energy light, helping us pin down the exact moments when neutron stars collide and forge new heavy elements. Together, these missions remind us that the universe is a dynamic, evolving, and deeply interconnected system. The gold in a wedding ring was forged in a neutron star merger, a process we study with StarBurst. The elements that make up our planet were distributed by supernova explosions, which we map with COSI. And the physics of how matter behaves under extreme gravitational forces is revealed by objects like the wind-stealing pulsars observed by XRISM. As these instruments continue to take data and make their way into the cosmos, they will undoubtedly continue to challenge our understanding, rewrite our textbooks, and show us that the dark, silent night sky is actually alive with a brilliant, invisible, high-energy glow.
